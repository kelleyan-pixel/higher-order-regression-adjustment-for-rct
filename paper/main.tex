\begin{abstract}
Regression adjustment is widely used to improve the precision of average treatment effect estimates from randomized controlled trials. Interacted regression weakly dominates simple regression in terms of its first-order behavior but, in 50-50 randomized trials, the asymptotic variance of both methods is the same and so relative performance is determined by higher-order behavior. Here, we conduct a higher order analysis to compare simple vs.~interacted regression adjustments in the equal-probability case. The relative performance of the methods depends on a number of factors including feature skewness, treatment-effect heterogeneity, heteroskedasticity, and nonlinear misspecification---and can favor either estimator. However, in some practically important settings, including when features are skewed and outlying feature values are associated with higher outcome noise, simple regression can outperform interacted regression by a material amount.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are\blfootnote{\hspace{-5.5mm}Code, simulation scripts and the computational certificates used in the proofs: \url{https://github.com/kelleyan-pixel/higher-order-regression-adjustment-for-rct}.} 
\blfootnote{\hspace{-5.5mm} Claude Opus 5.5 was used for code generation, including numerical simulation and computational proof design via SymPy \citep{sympy}; GPT-6 Astra was used for checking proofs and writing assistance throughout the appendix. }

fundamental to how causal effects are ascertained across a number of fields. One valuable attribute of RCTs is that they enable unbiased estimation of average treatment effects using simple methods such as the difference in means, without reliance on parametric assumptions \citep{imbens2015causal}. However, while the difference-in-means estimator is unbiased, this does not automatically make it the preferred approach; and in particular regression adjustments are often used to improve power. For example, the Food and Drug Administration issued recent guidance encouraging analysts to ``adjust for baseline covariates that are strongly associated with an outcome in a clinical trial population'' \citep[accompanying guidance snapshot]{FDA2023}.

The study of regression adjustments in RCTs has a long tradition in statistics. \citet{fisher1935design} used arguments rooted in parametric linear modeling to advocate for such regression adjustments. Fisher's analysis was later critiqued by \citet{Freedman2008}, who argued that reliance on linear models was misaligned with model-free guarantees sought in RCT inference. \citet{Lin2013}, however, responded to Freedman's critique by showing that regression adjustments do not require parametric assumptions to be near-unbiased for the average treatment effect. Furthermore, \citet{Lin2013} showed that regression adjustments with full treatment-control interaction weakly dominate both the difference in means estimator and Fisher's simple regression adjustment in terms of their leading-order (root-$n$ scale) errors. Lin's weak dominance result has led such treatment-interacted regressions to be often presented as a safe starting point for use of regression adjustments in RCTs \citep{li2017general,negi2021revisiting,Wager2025}.

A gap in this narrative, however, is that treatment-interacted regressions only strictly dominate simple regression in designs with randomization probability $\pi \neq 0.5$. In evenly randomized trials with $\pi = 0.5$, the first-order accuracy of both methods is equal for any data-generating distribution, i.e., first-order theory offers no guidance on which method to prefer. And, while $\pi = 0.5$ is of course a special case, this is an important special case in applications, and it is not a priori clear we should prefer interacted regression to simple regression here. For example, \citet{zhao2023covariate} argue based on simulation evidence and heuristics on the number of free parameters that simple regression may in practice be more stable than interacted regression with limited sample size---especially when there are multiple treatment levels.
Just to give a concrete example, suppose that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:warmup}
\log(X_i) \sim \nn\p{0, \, 1.1^2}, \ \ \ Y_i(w) = 0.9 X_i + w + (1 + X_i) U_i, \ \ \ U_i \sim \nn\p{0, \, 1}.
\end{equation}
Then, at sample size $n = 500$, interacted regression has 7\% higher mean-squared error for the average treatment effect than simple regression.

% Indeed, first-order equivalence can easily break down in finite sample sizes, as in the following illustrative example. Suppose a tech company runs an A/B test on a sample of its user base and adjusts for $X_i$, each user's spend in the prior time period. $X$ is strongly right-skewed because it is bounded below and a small number of heavy users account for the majority of the total. Suppose also that these superusers have more volatile outcomes, so that outcome noise increases with $X_i$, and that the treatment shifts every user's outcome by the same amount. Specifically, let the raw $X_i$ be marginally $\mathrm{LogNormal}(0,1.1^2)$ and let
% \[
% \sigma(X_i)=1+X_i,
% \qquad
% Y_i(0)=0.9\,X_i+\sigma(X_i)u_i,
% \qquad
% Y_i(1)=Y_i(0)+1,
% \]
% with $u_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)$. In simulation, interacted regression's MSE exceeds that of simple regression by $6.7\%$ at $n=500$, and the expansion-MSE deficiency is 83 observations; the corresponding actual-MSE interpretation requires the additional remainder conditions discussed in Section \ref{sec:asymptotics}. 

Motivated by this gap, we here conduct a higher-order asymptotic analysis of both simple and interacted regression adjustments in RCTs to elucidate relative merits of both approaches in the case where $\pi = 0.5$. We frame our findings in terms of asymptotic deficiency \citep{HodgesLehmann1970}. When two estimators have matching first-order behavior but one dominates the other on the second-order scale then, asymptotically, switching to the better one gives an accuracy boost that is equivalent to a fixed boost in sample size, which is referred to as the deficiency. Our main finding is that, while either the simple or interacted regression adjustment may have better second-order performance, this trade-off appears to favor simple regression in some realistic settings. 

As a practical extension of this theory, we study feasible inference choices for both estimators with heteroskedasticity-robust standard error estimates. In particular, we consider both interacted and simple regression point estimates in tandem with HC1 and jackknife/HC3 standard error estimates \citep{EfronStein1981,MacKinnonWhite1985}. For both point estimates, HC1 can undercover substantially under skewed, tail-heteroskedastic covariates. The jackknife improves coverage substantially in most designs but is still liable to undercover in cases of rare outliers or large HTE. In a typical low-HTE regime, simple regression combined with the jackknife consistently achieves at least nominal coverage with the smallest interval length.

\subsection{Related Work}
 
\paragraph{Regression adjustment in randomized experiments.}
The literature on regression adjustment in randomized experiments sits in two camps. The design-based literature treats the potential outcomes and covariates as fixed in a finite population, with randomness coming solely from treatment assignment. In this setting, \citet{Lin2013} shows that interacted regression weakly dominates both simple regression and difference-in-means in asymptotic efficiency. Meanwhile, \citet{zhao2023covariate} compare their finite sample behavior and allow for multi-armed and factorial experiments, postulating that interacted regression exhibits poor finite sample performance due to its additional model complexity. Another interesting but more distant line of work, including \citet{lei2021regression} and \citet{ChiangMatsushitaOtsu2026}, addresses bias and inference of the interacted estimator as $p$ grows with $n$.
 
By contrast, the superpopulation framework treats the experimental units as random draws from a larger population and studies repeated sampling over both the units and treatment assignment. Most past work here has focused on first-order comparisons. For instance, \citet{negi2021revisiting} show that the interacted regression estimator weakly dominates both difference in means and simple regression in this framework as well. We continue this line of work to formally investigate higher-order effects of both estimators, in particular to resolve the ambiguous optimality at $\pi=0.5$. Importantly, our goal is to characterize the higher-order behavior of the two estimators under IID sampling specifically, and it is possible that a design-based analysis yields different second-order results.
 
\paragraph{Higher-order asymptotic theory.}
First-order theory inherently cannot compare estimators with the same asymptotic variance, even though their finite-sample behavior may differ meaningfully. To address this, \citet{Nagar1959} introduced expansions of the higher-order moments of estimators, and \citet{Sargan1974} gave conditions for their validity; meanwhile Edgeworth expansions approximate their underlying distribution \citep{Sargan1976,BhattacharyaRao1976,Hall1992,Ghosh1994}. As we focus on the mean-squared error, we primarily build on the former tools, although the latter are useful for rigorous results on tail behavior and inference. \citet{HodgesLehmann1970} introduced asymptotic deficiency as a measure of the additional sample size required by the less accurate of two first-order equivalent procedures, and \citet{Rothenberg1984} emphasized that higher-order approximations should be consulted when competing estimators have the same limiting distribution. Parallel to our contribution, \citet{NeweySmith2004} present a higher-order comparison of the first-order equivalent general method of moments and generalized empirical likelihood estimators. \citet{Tan2014} studies the second-order properties of regression and likelihood calibration estimators in survey sampling and missing-data problems, finding that neither consistently dominates. \citet{ChiangMatsushitaOtsu2026} derive second-order stochastic expansions of regression-adjusted estimators to compare inference in a design-based, growing $p$ regime. However, to our knowledge we are the first to compare interacted and simple regression in a superpopulation setting using higher-order expansions.
 
\paragraph{Variance estimation.}
Under superpopulation sampling, the fixed-design based conventional sandwich variance for the interacted estimator ignores the variance of the sample covariate mean used for centering \citep[Section~5.3]{imbens2009recent}; see also \citet[Section~5]{Lin2013}. \citet{imbens2009recent} add an estimate of the omitted term, and \citet{negi2021revisiting} derive the asymptotic variance that accounts for it; we take this result for granted. General finite-sample behavior of heteroskedasticity-robust variance estimators is studied by \citet{EfronStein1981}, \citet{MacKinnonWhite1985}, \citet{ChesherJewitt1987}, \citet{LongErvin2000} and \citet{imbens2016robust}; the HC* nomenclature was introduced in \citet{MacKinnonWhite1985}. It is well known that despite their popularity in applied research, HC0 and HC1 are prone to undercovering in finite samples, and the statistics literature typically recommends switching to the jackknife (or HC3 in the \citet{MacKinnonWhite1985} terminology). \citet{lei2021regression} and \citet{ChiangMatsushitaOtsu2026} demonstrate this point specifically in the case of regression adjustment inference. Following this work, we present an empirical study of feasible inference using regression adjustment combined with robust standard errors, but we do not claim to contribute to this literature from a theoretical standpoint.

\section{Asymptotics of Regression Adjustment}
\label{sec:asymptotics}

We focus on completely randomized trials (CRTs) with causal effects formalized using the potential outcomes model \citep{imbens2015causal}. We assume the covariates $X_i\in \RR^p$ and potential outcomes $Y_i(0), \, Y_i(1) \in \RR$ are drawn IID from a distribution $P$, with average treatment effect $\tau = \EE{Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)}$. Both regression estimators below include an intercept, and interacted regression is evaluated at the sample mean of the covariates, so translating the covariates leaves both estimators unchanged; we therefore assume without loss of generality that $\EE{X}=0$. Treatment is assigned randomly conditional on the fixed treated group size $n_1=\sum_{i=1}^n W_i=\lfloor \pi n\rfloor$; treatment assignments are exchangeable but not independent.  The analyst observes $(X_i, \, Y_i, \, W_i)$ for $i = 1, \, \ldots, \, n$, where $Y_i = Y_i(W_i)$. We consider the following three estimators of the ATE:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:estimators}
\begin{split}
&\htau_{DM} = \frac{1}{\abs{\cb{i : W_i = 1}}} \sum_{\cb{i : W_i = 1}} Y_i - \frac{1}{\abs{\cb{i : W_i = 0}}} \sum_{\cb{i : W_i = 0}} Y_i, \\
&\htau_{REG} = \htau,  \text{ with }  Y_i \sim \alpha + X_i \beta + W_i \tau, \\
&\htau_{IREG} = \htau + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^n X_i \hdelta,  \text{ with } Y_i \sim \alpha + X_i \beta + W_i \tau+  W_i X_i \delta,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where, e.g., $Y_i \sim \alpha + X_i \beta + W_i \tau$ means we algorithmically run a regression of $Y_i$ against $X_i$ and $W_i$ with an intercept (without making any claims on well-specification of such a regression). To describe their first-order behavior it is helpful to define the best linear approximation to the conditional response function of the potential outcomes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:regr}
\cb{\alpha_{(w)}^*, \ \beta_{(w)}^*} =\argmin_{\alpha_{(w)},\,\beta_{(w)}} \cb{\EE{\p{Y_i(w) - X_i \beta_{(w)} - \alpha_{(w)}}^2}},
\end{equation}
together with the oracle residuals
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:epsi}
\varepsilon_i(w) = Y_i(w)-\alpha^*_{(w)}-X_i^\top\beta^*_{(w)},
\end{equation}
Writing $Z_i=\Sigma^{-1/2}X_i$ with $\Sigma=\Var{X_i}$, we have $\EEE[Z_i]=0$, $\EEE[Z_iZ_i^\top]=I_p$, $\EEE[Z_i\varepsilon_i(w)]=0$ and $\varepsilon_i = \varepsilon_i(W_i)$. The first-order behavior of the three estimators is well known \citep{negi2021revisiting}.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:first_order}
Suppose that $X_i$ and $Y_i(w)$ have finite second moments and that $\Var{X_i}$ is invertible. Then all three estimators above are asymptotically normal, $\sqrt{n}(\htau_\cdot - \tau) \Rightarrow \nn(0, \, V_\cdot)$, with limiting variances
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&V_{DM} = V_{IREG} +  \Var{X_i\p{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}} \, \beta_{(0)}^* + \sqrt{\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}} \, \beta_{(1)}^*}}, \\
&V_{REG} = V_{IREG} + \frac{\p{1 - 2\pi}^2}{\pi(1 - \pi)} \Var{X_i\p{\beta_{(1)}^* - \beta_{(0)}^*}}, \\
&V_{IREG} = \Var{X_i\p{\beta_{(1)}^* - \beta_{(0)}^*}} + \sum_{w = 0, \, 1} \frac{1}{\PP{W_i = w}} \EE{\p{Y_i(w) - X_i\beta_{(w)}^* - \alpha_{(w)}^*}^2}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

Thus $\htau_{IREG}$ weakly dominates both $\htau_{DM}$ and $\htau_{REG}$ at every $\pi$; its advantage over $\htau_{REG}$ is strict for generic distributions $P$ when $\pi\neq0.5$, while at $\pi = 0.5$ the two regression estimators have the same asymptotic variance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Vstar}
V^* = \Var{X_i\p{\beta_{(1)}^* - \beta_{(0)}^*}} + 2\EE{\varepsilon^2_i(1)} + 2\EE{\varepsilon^2_i(0)}.
\end{equation}
This is the setting we study. Because first-order theory is indifferent between the two methods, we turn to second-order asymptotics.

Importantly, the true MSE may not converge as $n\rightarrow\infty$ if the estimators have divergent higher moments. To address this issue, we consider a capped mean-squared error. For a fixed $c>0$, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:capped_mse}
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}\p{\htau} = \EE{\p{\htau - \tau}^2 \land c^2}
\end{equation}
This updated loss agrees with the regular squared error whenever $|\htau-\tau|\le c$ while bounding the contribution of unstable regression fits. We use the same fixed cutoff for both estimators. For this risk calculation, set an estimator to zero whenever its least-squares fit is not uniquely defined, including an empty treatment arm. Any measurable finite-valued completion gives the same expansion below. The cap is a loss convention depending on the target $\tau$, not a modification of the estimator. 

The proof of the following result is given in Appendix \ref{sec:proofs}. Appendix \ref{sec:numerical-verification} gives simulation-based comparisons of realized, uncapped MSE with the formula in a variety of settings.

\begin{theo}
\label{theo:deficiency}
Consider a CRT on $n$ units with $n_1 = \lfloor n/2\rfloor$, and suppose that
$(X_i, Y_i(0), Y_i(1))$ are IID from a distribution $P$, $\Sigma = \Var{X_i} \succ 0$, and
\[
\EEE\big[\|X_i\|^{12}\big], \ \EEE\big[|Y_i(w)|^{12}\big] < \infty \text{ for } w = 0, \, 1.
\]
Define the whitened features and corresponding effect-heterogeneity coefficients as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:whitening}
Z_i = \Sigma^{-1/2}X_i, \quad \Sigma = \Var{X_i},
\qquad
\gamma = \Sigma^{1/2}\p{\beta^*_{(1)}-\beta^*_{(0)}},
\end{equation}
and let
\[
K=\EEE[ZZ^\top\|Z\|^2],\qquad
S=\EEE[Z\|Z\|^2],\qquad
S_\gamma=\EEE[ZZ^\top(Z^\top\gamma)],
\]
\[
\mathcal E=\EEE[ZZ^\top\varepsilon],
\qquad
\mathcal E_{W}=\EEE[ZZ^\top(W-\tfrac12)\varepsilon] = \tfrac14\big(\EEE[ZZ^\top\varepsilon(1)]-\EEE[ZZ^\top\varepsilon(0)]\big).
\]
Here expectations involving $W$ use an auxiliary Bernoulli$(1/2)$ variable independent of the units, with $\varepsilon=\varepsilon(W)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\label{eq:Dp_general}
&\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}\p{\htau_{IREG}}-\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}\p{\htau_{REG}} = \frac{D_p}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}), \\
&D_p
=
-(2p+3)\|\gamma\|^2
+\gamma^\top K\gamma
-(\gamma^\top S)^2
-3\|S_\gamma\|_F^2\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad-16\tr(S_\gamma \mathcal E_W)
-8(\gamma^\top S)\tr(\mathcal E_W)
+8\gamma^\top \mathcal E_WS
+8\tr(\mathcal E^2)+8S^\top \EEE[Z\varepsilon^2].
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Consequently, when $V^*>0$, the asymptotic deficiency of IREG relative to REG, computed from the capped MSE, is $\Delta = D_p/V^*$ with $V^*$ as in \eqref{eq:Vstar}; the sample-size interpolation used here is specified in Appendix \ref{sec:proofs}.
\end{theo}

\begin{rema}[Bias and variance contributions]
\label{rema:bias}
One may naturally be interested in how the overall MSE difference decomposes into bias and variance. Writing $e_{j,c}=\max\{-c,\min\{\htau_j-\tau,c\}\}$, define
\[
\mathrm{Bias}_j=\EEE[e_{j,c}],
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}_j=\Var{e_{j,c}},
\qquad
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}(\htau_j)=\mathrm{Var}_j+\mathrm{Bias}_j^2.
\]
Evaluating the leading bias term for the two estimators (Lemma \ref{lemm:bias} in Appendix \ref{sec:proofs}) gives
\[
\mathrm{Bias}_{\REG}
= -\frac{4\tr(\mathcal E_W)+\gamma^\top S}{n}+O(n^{-2}),
\qquad
\mathrm{Bias}_{\IREG}
= -\frac{4\tr(\mathcal E_W)}{n}+O(n^{-2}),
\]
so that
$n^2\{\mathrm{Bias}^2_{\IREG}-\mathrm{Bias}^2_{\REG}\}
=-(\gamma^\top S)^2-8(\gamma^\top S)\tr(\mathcal E_W)$
to leading order, indeed directly reproducing two terms;
the remaining terms are due to variance.
\end{rema}


The expression for $D_p$ combines four key blocks: a covariate shape block scaled by the linear HTE $\gamma$; terms crossing $\gamma$ with residual asymmetry; residual heteroskedasticity; and nonlinear misspecification. No single term determines the sign and either method can come up on top---even when the population linear slopes coincide (i.e., when $\gamma = 0$). When $\gamma = 0$, the expression in \eqref{eq:Dp_general} reduces to
\[
D_p=8\tr(\mathcal E^2)+8S^\top \EEE[Z\varepsilon^2],
\]
and while $\tr(\mathcal E^2) = \|\mathcal E\|_F^2 \geq 0$, the term $S^\top \EEE[Z\varepsilon^2]$ can have either sign, so $\Delta$ can be positive or negative even when $\gamma = 0$.
To interpret the scale of $\Delta$ write
\[
R^2_\tau
=
\frac{\|\gamma\|^2}{\|\gamma\|^2+4\EEE[\varepsilon_i^2]}
= \frac{\|\gamma\|^2}{V^*},
\]
which, when $V^*>0$, quantifies the share of the first-order variance $V^*$ attributable to linear treatment-effect heterogeneity.
% the variance share due to HTE as.
% Consider the balanced doubly robust pseudo-outcome built from interacted linear model $\mu_w(x)=\alpha^*_{(w)}+x^\top\beta^*_{(w)}$,
% \[
% \phi_i
% =
% 2W_i\{Y_i-\mu_1(X_i)\}
% -
% 2(1-W_i)\{Y_i-\mu_0(X_i)\}
% +
% \mu_1(X_i)-\mu_0(X_i).
% \]
% Then $\phi_i-\tau = \gamma^\top Z_i+\xi_i$ with $\xi_i = 2W_i\varepsilon_i(1)-2(1-W_i)\varepsilon_i(0)$; the normal equations give $\EEE[Z_i\xi_i]=0$, $\Var{\xi_i}=4\EE{\varepsilon_i^2}$ and $\Var{\gamma^\top Z_i}=\|\gamma\|^2$, so $R^2_\tau$ is the population $R^2$ from linearly predicting the treatment-effect pseudo-outcome from $Z_i$. 

\begin{coro}
\label{coro:homosk}
Under the conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}, suppose furthermore that the potential outcomes satisfy a well-specified linear model with homoskedastic errors, $\EE{\varepsilon_i(w)\cond X_i}=0$ and $\EE{\varepsilon_i^2(w)\cond X_i}=\sigma_w^2$ for $w\in\{0,1\}$. Then $\mathcal E = \mathcal E_W = 0$ and $\EEE[Z\varepsilon^2]=0$. For $\gamma\neq0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:homosk_deficiency}
\Delta
=
R_\tau^2
\left[
-(2p+3)
+
\frac{\gamma^\top K\gamma-(\gamma^\top S)^2-3\|S_\gamma\|_F^2}{\|\gamma\|^2}
\right].
\end{equation}
In the Gaussian sub-case $K = (p+2)I_p$, $S=0$ and $S_\gamma = 0$, so that $\Delta = -(p+1)R_\tau^2$.
If $\gamma=0$, the second-order MSE difference vanishes for any covariate distribution; when $V^*>0$, $R_\tau^2=0$ and $\Delta=0$.
\end{coro}

In the Gaussian homoskedastic case, the second-order coefficient favors IREG and grows linearly in the number of covariates, with $p$ fixed as $n\to\infty$. This complicates the parameter-count heuristic of \citet{zhao2023covariate}, showing that the finite sample advantage of either estimator cannot be reduced to any simple feature of the DGP.

However, the following corollary sheds some light on why REG has been observed to outperform IREG to perform worse in many real-life datasets, or in examples such as \eqref{eq:warmup}.

\begin{coro}
\label{coro:mult_normproportional}
Under the conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}, suppose furthermore that the potential outcomes follow
\[
Y_i(w) = \alpha^*_{(w)} + X_i^\top\beta^*_{(w)} + \eta_i\|Z_i\|,
\]
where $\EE{\eta_i}=0$, $\EE{\eta_i^2}=\nu^2$ and $\eta_i$ is independent of $(Z_i,W_i)$. Then $\EE{\varepsilon_i(w)\cond Z_i}=0$ and $\EE{\varepsilon_i^2(w)\cond Z_i}=\nu^2\|Z_i\|^2$, where $\|\cdot\|$ is the Euclidean norm, so that $\EE{Z_i\varepsilon_i^2(w)}=\nu^2S$ and $\EE{\varepsilon_i^2}=\nu^2p$. For $\gamma\neq0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mult}
\Delta
=
R_\tau^2
\left[
-(2p+3)
+
\frac{\gamma^\top K\gamma-(\gamma^\top S)^2-3\|S_\gamma\|_F^2}{\|\gamma\|^2}
\right]
+
\frac{8\nu^2\,S^\top S}{\|\gamma\|^2+4\nu^2 p}.
\end{equation}
When $\gamma=0$ and $\nu^2>0$, the deficiency is
$\Delta=2\|S\|^2/p$, which is nonnegative and is strictly
positive whenever $S\neq0$.
\end{coro}


It is useful to consider how the terms in \eqref{eq:mult} compare in practice. In many applications the linear HTE signal is small relative to the residual departure from the regression model, so $R_\tau^2$ is small and the first summand rarely dominates unless its bracket is large. The bracketed component is of order $p$ in the leading cases---it equals $-(p+1)$ under Gaussian covariates---and its moment terms grow with the kurtosis and skewness of the underlying covariates, so it is sensitive to departures from Gaussianity in either direction.

The remaining term is nonnegative, since $8\nu^2\|S\|^2/(\|\gamma\|^2+4\nu^2p)\geq0$, and for $\nu>0$ it vanishes if and only if $S=\EEE[Z\|Z\|^2]=0$. Right-skewed covariates with a long upper tail, such as measures of user activity, engagement or spending, can produce a large third-moment vector $S$ after centering and whitening, in which case this term can dominate the first summand. The scalar case makes this transparent: with $p=1$, Corollary \ref{coro:mult_normproportional} gives
\[
\Delta
=
R_\tau^2\cb{\EE{Z_i^4}-4\EE{Z_i^3}^2-5}
+
\frac{8\nu^2\EE{Z_i^3}^2}{\gamma^2+4\nu^2}.
\]
Using Pearson's inequality $\EE{Z_i^4}\geq \EE{Z_i^3}^2+1$ \citep[see, e.g.,][]{rohatgi1989sharp} and $8\nu^2/(\gamma^2+4\nu^2) = 2(1-R^2_\tau)$,
\[
\Delta
\geq
-4R_\tau^2 + (2-5R_\tau^2)\EE{Z_i^3}^2 .
\]
So when $R_\tau^2 < 2/5$ the deficiency is positive as soon as $\EE{Z_i^3}^2 > 4R^2_\tau/(2-5R^2_\tau)$, and the lower bound grows linearly in $\EE{Z_i^3}^2$. 

\section{Feasible Inference}
\label{sec:feasible}

While the above results concern the precision of the point estimates provided by REG and IREG, in practice we are also interested in the validity of the resulting confidence intervals---which now depends jointly on how we estimate the standard error. As before, we operate in an assumption-lean framework, only considering estimators that are robust to misspecification of the underlying linear model.

%\subsection{Implementation and variance estimators}
%\label{sec:standard}

We fit REG and IREG as in the R package \texttt{estimatr} \citep{estimatr}: REG is \texttt{lm\char`\_robust(y \textasciitilde{} w + x)}, and IREG is \texttt{lm\char`\_lin(y \textasciitilde{} w, covariates = \textasciitilde{} x)}, with \texttt{se\char`\_type = "HC1"} (the package default is HC2). Because we operate in a superpopulation framework while these implementations are for fixed design sampling, we add a plug-in estimate of the variance of the sample covariate mean used for centering (\citealp[Section~5.3]{imbens2009recent}; \citealp{negi2021revisiting}):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vsp}
\hat G=\hdelta^\top\hat\Sigma_X\hdelta,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{SP}}(\htau_\IREG)=\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HC1}}(\htau_\IREG)+\frac{\hat G}{n},
\end{equation}
where $\hdelta$ is the vector of interaction coefficients and $\hat\Sigma_X$ the sample covariance of the covariates; $\hat G$ estimates $G=\|\gamma\|^2$. Because $\hdelta$ is itself noisy, $\hat G$ is biased upward, by about $\tr\{\mathrm{Var}(\hdelta)\,\Sigma\}$.

We choose to investigate HC1 as a popular benchmark: it is Stata's default and used in $98.1\%$ of 40,571 robust Stata regressions in reproduction packages of leading economics journals \citep{Kranz2024}. We compare it with a leverage-aware alternative, the delete-one jackknife applied to the final estimate \citep{MacKinnonWhite1985}. Let $\htau_{(-i)}$ be the estimator recomputed on the sample with unit $i$ removed---both arm-specific fits are refitted and, for IREG, the covariates are recentred at the leave-one-out mean $\bar X_{(-i)}$---the jackknife variance estimate is then given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jk}
\hat V_{\mathrm{JK}}
=
\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\big(\htau_{(-i)}-\bar\htau\big)^2,
\qquad
\bar\htau = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \htau_{(-i)} .
\end{equation}
Because the jackknife operates on the final treatment effect estimate, it accounts for the sampling variation of the covariate mean and thus requires no superpopulation correction. Thus for IREG we report HC1 with the plug-in correction \eqref{eq:vsp} alongside the jackknife; for REG, we directly report HC1 and jackknife. When removing unit $i$ leaves a rank-deficient design (unit $i$ has leverage one in its fit), $\htau_{(-i)}$ is undefined; we then omit it and apply \eqref{eq:jk} to the remaining $n'$ leave-one-out estimates, with $n'$ in place of $n$. All intervals use $t$ critical values with $n-k$ degrees of freedom, where $k$ is the number of regression coefficients.

\section{Proofs}
\label{sec:proofs}

The goal of this section is to derive the constant $D_p$ in Theorem
\ref{theo:deficiency}. The argument has three parts. First, we express a
completely randomized trial as two independent samples and obtain an
MSE expansion for a fixed treatment fraction. Second, we calculate the
second-order constant under Bernoulli assignment. Third, we remove the
effect of random treatment imbalance to recover the constant for a
balanced trial. The remaining lemmas translate the answer into matrix
notation and establish the identities used in the numerical checks.

Throughout this appendix, $j\in\{\REG,\IREG\}$ identifies the
estimator and $c>0$ is the fixed loss cutoff in \eqref{eq:capped_mse}.
The subscripts $\Bern$ and $(\CRT,r)$ indicate, respectively,
independent Bernoulli$(1/2)$ assignment and complete randomization
with $m=rn$ treated units. Expectations under these designs are
written $\EEE_\Bern$ and $\EEE_{\CRT,r}$. In particular,
\[
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\mathcal D}(\htau_j)
=\EEE_{\mathcal D}[\min\{(\htau_j-\tau)^2,c^2\}],
\qquad \mathcal D\in\{\Bern,(\CRT,r)\}.
\]
An omitted fraction in a $\CRT$ subscript means
$r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n$. Estimators use the finite-valued
completion specified after \eqref{eq:capped_mse}.

\begin{defi}[Taylor-polynomial MSE]
\label{def:msexp}
Let $\bar{\mathcal V}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathcal V_i$ and
$\hat\tau=g(\bar{\mathcal V})$ locally, where $g$ is smooth
near $\mu=\EEE[\mathcal V_i]$ and $g(\mu)=\tau$. Put
$d=\bar{\mathcal V}-\mu$, $a_i=\partial_i g(\mu)$,
$H_{ij}=\partial_{ij}g(\mu)$ and
$A_{3,ijk}=\partial_{ijk}g(\mu)$. Repeated coordinate indices in
this definition are summed from $1$ to the dimension of $\mathcal V_i$.
The cubic Taylor polynomial is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hall_expansion}
\tilde\tau_n
=\tau+a_i d_i+\tfrac12H_{ij}d_i d_j
+\tfrac16A_{3,ijk}d_i d_j d_k,
\end{equation}
and its mean-squared error is defined by
\[
\MSEexp(\hat\tau):=\EEE[(\tilde\tau_n-\tau)^2].
\]
The superscript $\mathrm{exp}$ labels the Taylor expansion; this is
the exact squared-error expectation of the polynomial, not of
$\hat\tau$ itself. It depends on the coefficient map and raw-moment
coordinates specified below.

For fixed $r=m/n\in(0,1)$, let $U_i^{(w)}$ contain $X_i$, $Y_i(w)$,
the distinct entries of $X_iX_i^\top$, and $X_iY_i(w)$, and put
$\mu_w=\EEE[U_i^{(w)}]$. Let $\bar U_w$ be its sample mean in
arm $w$. The local coefficient map is
$\hat\tau_j=h_{j,r}(\bar U_1,\bar U_0)$, given explicitly in
Lemma \ref{lemm:two_sample}. Write $\mathcal P_{j,r}$ for its
cubic Taylor polynomial at $\mu=(\mu_1,\mu_0)$, using the displayed
derivative formula with $d=(\bar U_1-\mu_1,\bar U_0-\mu_0)$, and define
\[
\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\hat\tau_j)
=\EEE_{\CRT,r}[(\mathcal P_{j,r}-\tau)^2].
\]
We abbreviate $\mathcal P_{j,r}$ to $\mathcal P_r$ when the
estimator $j$ is fixed.

For Bernoulli assignment use the IID vector
$\mathcal V_i=(W_i,W_iU_i^{(1)},(1-W_i)U_i^{(0)})$, with mean
$\mu=(1/2,\mu_1/2,\mu_0/2)$. For its three blocks $v=(v_W,v_1,v_0)$,
the local coefficient map is
\[
g_j(v)=h_{j,v_W}\big(v_1/v_W,v_0/(1-v_W)\big).
\]
Let $\tilde\tau_{n,j}$ be the cubic polynomial
\eqref{eq:hall_expansion} for this map and define
\[
\MSEexp_\Bern(\hat\tau_j)
=\EEE_\Bern[(\tilde\tau_{n,j}-\tau)^2].
\]
The maps need only be defined near their population means; their
Taylor polynomials are defined for every sample-moment vector,
including samples on which a regression fit is undefined.
\end{defi}

Lemma \ref{lemm:localization} shows that Taylor-polynomial MSE
agrees with the capped MSE through order $n^{-2}$, with an
$O(n^{-3})$ difference, under the sampling and moment conditions of
Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}. No inverse-Gram moment assumption is
needed for this capped loss. Ordinary, uncapped MSE requires additional
tail control. For example, if the completed estimator has finite
second moment and
\[
\|\htau_j-\tilde\tau_{n,j}\|_{L^2}=o(n^{-3/2}),
\]
under Bernoulli assignment, then $\|\tilde\tau_{n,j}-\tau\|_{L^2}=O(n^{-1/2})$ and
Cauchy--Schwarz give an $o(n^{-2})$ difference between its ordinary
MSE and $\MSEexp$. This sufficient condition is not implied by the
population moment assumptions. For fixed-fraction CRT, the same
sufficient condition uses $\mathcal P_{j,r}$ in place of
$\tilde\tau_{n,j}$.

Statements about deficiency require $V^*>0$. We interpret a
noninteger sample-size shift by linear interpolation of the capped
risks between even sample sizes. On that sequence, each risk has the
form $V^*/n+c_j(1/2)/n^2+O(n^{-3})$, where $c_j(r)$ is the
second-order coefficient defined by Lemma \ref{lemm:conditional},
and hence
\[
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\IREG}(n+\Delta)
-\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\REG}(n)=O(n^{-3}),
\qquad
\Delta=\frac{c_\IREG(1/2)-c_\REG(1/2)}{V^*}.
\]
Here $\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_j(n)$ denotes the capped risk
of estimator $j$ at balanced sample size $n$, and noninteger
arguments denote its linear interpolation. An integer
implementation entails rounding. For odd $n$, the individual
$n^{-2}$ risk coefficients can have a common parity correction;
the same-$n$ difference in Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} is unchanged
through that order. If $V^*=0$, the risk-difference identity still
holds, but division by $V^*$ does not define a deficiency.

Population expectations involving $W$ in the second-order constants
are evaluated at the nominal allocation: an auxiliary
Bernoulli$(1/2)$ variable independent of the potential outcomes and
covariates. This convention also applies when the realized CRT has
odd $n$, with treatment fraction $\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n$. The effect of
that $O(n^{-1})$ imbalance on the same-$n$ risk difference is included in the remainder.

We use $G=\|\gamma\|^2$. In covariate-moment tensor formulas, indices such as
$i,j,k,l,a,b,c,d$ range from $1$ to $p$ and refer to covariate
coordinates, rather than experimental units. Repeated indices are
summed over unless stated otherwise. In particular, $\delta_{jk}$ is
the Kronecker delta. Indices of the raw-moment vector $\mathcal V$
instead range over the coordinates of that vector; an estimator
subscript $j\in\{\REG,\IREG\}$ is never summed.

\subsection{Two independent samples and a fixed-fraction expansion}

The first lemma explains why superpopulation sampling simplifies the
complete-randomization calculation. Once the treatment assignment is
fixed, the two arms consist of disjoint sets of independent units.

We use the arm raw-moment vectors and their means from
Definition \ref{def:msexp}.

\begin{lemm}[Two-sample representation]
\label{lemm:two_sample}
Assume the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem
\ref{theo:deficiency}. Let the assignment vector $W$ be
independent of the sampled units, with $\sum_iW_i=m$ and $0<m<n$. Conditionally on $W$, the treated observations $\{(X_i,Y_i(1)):W_i=1\}$ and the control observations $\{(X_i,Y_i(0)):W_i=0\}$ are independent IID samples of sizes $m$ and $n-m$ from the laws of $(X,Y(1))$ and $(X,Y(0))$ respectively. 

Furthermore, wherever the required fits are uniquely defined, both $\htau_\REG$ and $\htau_\IREG$ are of the form $h_{j,r}(\bar U_1,\bar U_0)$ with $r=m/n$, where $h_{j,r}$ is infinitely differentiable in a neighborhood of $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$ and its derivatives at $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$ are rational functions of $r$ without poles in $(0,1)$.
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
Conditioning on $W$ fixes two disjoint index sets. Independence of
$W$ and the sampled units leaves the unit distributions unchanged;
independence across units then gives independence both within and
between the arms.

To establish the second claim, note that the coordinates of $\bar U_w$
determine all per-arm means, second moments, and cross-moments needed
for least squares. Both estimators are rational functions of these
coordinates. To see this explicitly, let
$C_w=\overline{XX^\top}_w-\bar X_w\bar X_w^\top$ and
$s_w=\overline{XY}_w-\bar X_w\bar Y_w$. Define the arm-specific
and pooled within-arm slopes by
\[
\hat\beta_w=C_w^{-1}s_w,
\qquad
\hat\beta_{\rm pool}(r)
=\{rC_1+(1-r)C_0\}^{-1}\{rs_1+(1-r)s_0\}.
\]
Writing $d_X=\bar X_1-\bar X_0$, the two coefficient maps are
\[
\begin{aligned}
h_{\REG,r}(\bar U_1,\bar U_0)
&=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0-d_X^\top\hat\beta_{\rm pool}(r),\\
h_{\IREG,r}(\bar U_1,\bar U_0)
&=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0
-d_X^\top\{(1-r)\hat\beta_1+r\hat\beta_0\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The second formula evaluates each arm's fitted regression at
$\bar X=r\bar X_1+(1-r)\bar X_0$. At $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$,
$d_X=0$, both maps equal $\tau$, and each population covariance
matrix is $\Sigma\succ0$. This gives smoothness and the stated
dependence on $r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemm}[Localization for capped loss and clipped errors]
\label{lemm:localization}
Under the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem
\ref{theo:deficiency}, for any fixed $c>0$ and
$\eta\in(0,1/2)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:capped_transfer}
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\CRT,r}(\htau_j)
=\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\htau_j)+O(n^{-3}),
\end{equation}
uniformly for $r=m/n\in[1/2-\eta,1/2+\eta]$. Also, with
$e_{j,c}=\max\{-c,\min\{\htau_j-\tau,c\}\}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clipped_bias_transfer}
\EEE[e_{j,c}]=\EEE[\mathcal P_r-\tau]+O(n^{-2})
\end{equation}
uniformly on the same interval. The analogous statements hold under
independent Bernoulli$(1/2)$ assignment with the unit-level cubic
Taylor polynomial $\tilde\tau_{n,j}$.
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
Each coordinate of $U^{(w)}$ has a finite sixth moment: for example,
\[
\EEE|X_kY(w)|^6
\le\{\EEE|X_k|^{12}\,\EEE|Y(w)|^{12}\}^{1/2},
\]
and the same argument applies to $X_kX_l$. For
$\mu=(\mu_1,\mu_0)$ and $d=(\bar U_1-\mu_1,\bar U_0-\mu_0)$, centered sample-mean moment
counting and independence of the arms give, uniformly over these
fractions,
\[
\EEE\|d\|^6=O(n^{-3}),\qquad
\EEE\|d\|^4=O(n^{-2}).
\]
Indeed, in any product of centered observations a sample index
appearing only once has zero expectation. At most three distinct
indices can therefore contribute to a degree-six moment. The same
count shows that the expectation of every signed degree-five
monomial in $d$ is $O(n^{-3})$; an absolute fifth-moment bound alone
would not give this order.

By Lemma \ref{lemm:two_sample} and compactness of the fraction
interval, there is a fixed $\delta>0$ such that on
$\mathcal A=\{\|d\|\le\delta\}$ both regression maps are smooth,
their derivatives through order six are uniformly bounded, and
$|h_{j,r}(\mu+d)-\tau|\le c$. Thus the cap is inactive there and
$\Pr(\mathcal A^c)=O(n^{-3})$. Write $q_k$ for the homogeneous
degree-$k$ Taylor term of $h_{j,r}-\tau$. Taylor expansion of the
squared error through degree five gives, on $\mathcal A$,
\[
(h_{j,r}-\tau)^2
=q_1^2+2q_1q_2+q_2^2+2q_1q_3
+2q_1q_4+2q_2q_3+O(\|d\|^6).
\]
For every $0\le k\le6$,
\[
\EEE[\|d\|^k\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A^c}]
\le\delta^{k-6}\EEE\|d\|^6=O(n^{-3}).
\]
Consequently each displayed polynomial can be integrated over the
whole sample space with only $O(n^{-3})$ error. The two degree-five
terms have signed expectations $O(n^{-3})$, and
$\EEE[q_3^2]=O(n^{-3})$. Comparing with
$\EEE[(q_1+q_2+q_3)^2]$, and bounding the capped loss on
$\mathcal A^c$ by $c^2$, proves \eqref{eq:capped_transfer}.
This argument also shows why any finite-valued completion on
undefined fits gives the same expansion: all such fits lie outside
$\mathcal A$.

For clipped bias, the clipping is again inactive on $\mathcal A$.
The cubic Taylor remainder there is $O(\|d\|^4)$ and hence has
expectation $O(n^{-2})$. The clipped error is bounded by $c$ off
$\mathcal A$, and the off-event expectations of the cubic polynomial
are $O(n^{-3})$ by the preceding bound. This proves
\eqref{eq:clipped_bias_transfer}.

Under Bernoulli assignment one may use
$\mathcal V_i=(W_i,W_iU_i^{(1)},(1-W_i)U_i^{(0)})$.
These IID raw-moment vectors have finite sixth moments. At their
population mean both treatment probabilities are $1/2$ and the
population Gram matrices are invertible. The same argument applies
to the smooth coefficient maps in these coordinates, with
$d=\bar{\mathcal V}-\EEE\mathcal V$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemm}[Conditional expansion]
\label{lemm:conditional}
Under the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}, let $\mathcal P_r$ be the third-order Taylor polynomial of $h_{j,r}$
at $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$ and define
$\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\htau_j)=\EEE[(\mathcal P_r-\tau)^2]$. For every
$\eta\in(0,\frac12)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditional_expansion}
\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\hat{\tau}_{j})
=
\frac{V_j(r)}{n}+\frac{c_j(r)}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}),
\qquad
j\in\{\REG,\IREG\},
\end{equation}
uniformly in $r=m/n\in[\frac12-\eta,\frac12+\eta]$, with $V_j$ and $c_j$ infinitely differentiable in $r$. The same expansion holds with $\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\CRT,r}$ in place of $\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}$.
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
Fix an estimator $j\in\{\REG,\IREG\}$ and an interval
$I_\eta=[\frac12-\eta,\frac12+\eta]$ with $0<\eta<1/2$.
The arm sizes are $m=rn$ and $n-m=(1-r)n$. We first expand the
squared Taylor polynomial, then determine which sample-mean moments
can contribute at orders $n^{-1}$ and $n^{-2}$.

\emph{Step 1: expand the squared estimation error.}
Write $d_1=\bar U_1-\mu_1$ and $d_0=\bar U_0-\mu_0$. Then $h_{j,r}(\mu_1,\mu_0)=\tau$, and the third-order Taylor polynomial of $h_{j,r}$ at $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$ can be written
\[
\mathcal P_r-\tau=q_1+q_2+q_3,
\]
where $q_k$ is a polynomial of degree $k$ in the coordinates of $(d_1,d_0)$ whose coefficients are the $k$th derivatives of $h_{j,r}$ at $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$ divided by $k!$. By Lemma \ref{lemm:two_sample}, each of these coefficients is a rational function of $r$ without poles in $(0,1)$. Expanding the square,
\[
\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\htau_j)
=\EEE[q_1^2]+2\EEE[q_1q_2]+\big(\EEE[q_2^2]+2\EEE[q_1q_3]\big)+2\EEE[q_2q_3]+\EEE[q_3^2].
\]
\emph{Step 2: justify the required moments and separate the arms.}
After expanding the polynomials, each expectation is a linear
combination of mixed moments of the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mixed_moment}
\EEE\Big[\prod_{u=1}^{a}d_{1,k_u}\prod_{u=1}^{b}d_{0,l_u}\Big],\qquad 2\le a+b\le 6,
\end{equation}
with coefficients that are products of two Taylor coefficients, and hence rational in $r$ without poles in $(0,1)$. Each coordinate of $U^{(w)}$ is one of $X_k$, $Y(w)$, $X_kX_l$ or $X_kY(w)$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $\EEE|X_kX_l|^6\le(\EEE|X_k|^{12}\,\EEE|X_l|^{12})^{1/2}$, and similarly for $X_kY(w)$, so the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} give $\EEE\|U^{(w)}\|^6<\infty$. By H\"older's inequality, every mixed moment of order at most six of the coordinates of $U^{(w)}$ is then finite.

By Lemma \ref{lemm:two_sample}, conditionally on $W$ the treated and control observations are independent IID samples of sizes $m$ and $n-m$. In particular $d_1$ and $d_0$ are independent, and \eqref{eq:mixed_moment} factors as
\[
\EEE\Big[\prod_{u=1}^{a}d_{1,k_u}\Big]\;\EEE\Big[\prod_{u=1}^{b}d_{0,l_u}\Big].
\]
\emph{Step 3: count powers of the arm size.}
Consider the treated-arm factor. The control-arm calculation is the
same, with $U^{(0)}$ and $n-m$ in place of $U^{(1)}$ and $m$.
Write $\kappa_{k_1\cdots k_s}$ for the joint cumulant of $(U^{(1)}_{k_1},\ldots,U^{(1)}_{k_s})$. By the IID property,
\[
\operatorname{cum}(d_{1,k_1},\ldots,d_{1,k_s})
=m^{-s}\sum_{i:W_i=1}\operatorname{cum}\big(U^{(1)}_{i,k_1},\ldots,U^{(1)}_{i,k_s}\big)
=m^{1-s}\,\kappa_{k_1\cdots k_s}\qquad(2\le s\le6),
\]
while the first-order cumulants of $d_1$ are zero. The moment--cumulant formula writes $\EEE[\prod_{u=1}^a d_{1,k_u}]$ as a sum over set partitions $\pi$ of $\{1,\ldots,a\}$ of $\prod_{B\in\pi}\operatorname{cum}(d_{1,k_u}:u\in B)$. A partition with a singleton block contributes zero. A partition into blocks of size at least two contributes a product of cumulants of $U^{(1)}$ times $m^{\sum_{B\in\pi}(1-|B|)}=m^{-(a-|\pi|)}$, and such a partition has $1\le|\pi|\le\lfloor a/2\rfloor$. Hence, for $2\le a\le6$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mean_moment}
\EEE\Big[\prod_{u=1}^{a}d_{1,k_u}\Big]=\sum_{s=\lceil a/2\rceil}^{a-1}\frac{M^{(1)}_s}{m^{s}},
\end{equation}
where each $M^{(1)}_s$ is a finite sum of products of cumulants of $U^{(1)}$ and does not depend on $m$; the moment equals $0$ for $a=1$ and $1$ for $a=0$. For example,
\[
\begin{gathered}
\EEE[d_{1,k}d_{1,l}]=\frac{\kappa_{kl}}{m},
\qquad
\EEE[d_{1,k}d_{1,l}d_{1,q}]=\frac{\kappa_{klq}}{m^2},
\\
\EEE[d_{1,k}d_{1,l}d_{1,q}d_{1,v}]=\frac{\kappa_{kl}\kappa_{qv}+\kappa_{kq}\kappa_{lv}+\kappa_{kv}\kappa_{lq}}{m^2}+\frac{\kappa_{klqv}}{m^3}.
\end{gathered}
\]
These identities hold exactly for every $m$; no approximation is involved.

\emph{Step 4: collecting powers of $n$.}
Substitute $m=rn$ and $n-m=(1-r)n$. By \eqref{eq:mean_moment}, a mixed moment \eqref{eq:mixed_moment} with $a$ treated and $b$ control coordinates vanishes if $a=1$ or $b=1$, and otherwise is a finite sum of terms
\[
\frac{M^{(1)}_sM^{(0)}_t}{r^{s}(1-r)^{t}}\;n^{-(s+t)},
\qquad
\lceil a/2\rceil\le s,\quad \lceil b/2\rceil\le t,
\]
with $s=0$ and $M^{(1)}_0=1$ when $a=0$, and $t=0$ and $M^{(0)}_0=1$ when $b=0$. Its power of $1/n$ is therefore at least $\lceil a/2\rceil+\lceil b/2\rceil\ge\lceil (a+b)/2\rceil$, and at most $5$. Combining these moment identities with the squared expansion in Step 1,
\[
\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\htau_j)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{5}\frac{e_\ell(r)}{n^{\ell}},
\]
where each $e_\ell$ is a finite sum of products of Taylor coefficients, cumulants of $U^{(1)}$ and $U^{(0)}$, and powers of $r^{-1}$ and $(1-r)^{-1}$. Each $e_\ell$ is thus rational in $r$ without poles in $(0,1)$, and in particular infinitely differentiable and bounded on $I_\eta$. The expansion is exact: a polynomial in $1/n$, not an asymptotic series.

The coefficient of $n^{-2}$ does not require every term in the square.
The following degree count identifies the terms that matter. The degree-two term $\EEE[q_1^2]$ contributes to $\ell=1$ only:
\[
\EEE[q_1^2]
=\frac{g_1^\top\Var{U^{(1)}}g_1}{m}+\frac{g_0^\top\Var{U^{(0)}}g_0}{n-m}
=\frac1n\left\{\frac{g_1^\top\Var{U^{(1)}}g_1}{r}+\frac{g_0^\top\Var{U^{(0)}}g_0}{1-r}\right\},
\]
where $g_w$ is the gradient of $h_{j,r}$ with respect to $\bar U_w$ at $(\mu_1,\mu_0)$. The degree-three term $2\EEE[q_1q_2]$ contributes to $\ell=2$ only, through third cumulants. The degree-four terms $\EEE[q_2^2]+2\EEE[q_1q_3]$ contribute to $\ell=2$ and $\ell=3$. The degree-five and degree-six terms contribute only to $\ell\ge3$. Hence
\[
V_j(r)=e_1(r),\qquad c_j(r)=e_2(r),
\]
where $c_j(r)$ collects the $n^{-2}$ parts of $2\EEE[q_1q_2]$, $\EEE[q_2^2]$ and $2\EEE[q_1q_3]$.

\emph{Step 5: make the remainder bound uniform.}
Because $r$ stays away from $0$ and $1$, the remaining coefficients
are bounded independently of the treatment fraction. Specifically,
for every $n\ge1$ and admissible $r\in I_\eta$,
\[
\left|\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}(\htau_j)-\frac{V_j(r)}{n}-\frac{c_j(r)}{n^2}\right|
=\left|\sum_{\ell=3}^{5}\frac{e_\ell(r)}{n^{\ell}}\right|
\le\frac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{\ell=3}^{5}\sup_{r'\in I_\eta}|e_\ell(r')|.
\]
The right-hand side is finite because each $e_\ell$ is continuous on the compact interval $I_\eta$. This is the claimed $O(n^{-3})$ bound, uniformly in $r\in I_\eta$. Lemma \ref{lemm:localization} transfers it to the capped risk.
\end{proof}

\subsection{From Bernoulli assignment to balanced complete randomization}

Bernoulli assignment makes the unit-level moment calculation convenient,
but it also makes the treatment fraction random. The next lemma permits
us to average the fixed-fraction expansion over that random fraction.
The theorem that follows identifies the correction for fixing the arm
sizes instead.

\begin{lemm}[Bernoulli and conditional expansions]
\label{lemm:bern_conditional}
Under the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem
\ref{theo:deficiency}, with
$W_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)$
independent of the units, let $\bar W=n^{-1}\sum_iW_i$.
For every $\eta\in(0,1/2)$,
\[
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_\Bern(\htau_j)
=\EEE\Big[
\overline{\operatorname{MSE}}_{\CRT,\bar W}(\htau_j)
\mathbf 1\{|\bar W-\tfrac12|\le\eta\}\Big]
+O(e^{-2n\eta^2}).
\]
Consequently,
\[
\MSEexp_\Bern(\htau_j)
=\EEE\Big[\MSEexp_{\CRT,\bar W}(\htau_j)
\mathbf 1\{|\bar W-\tfrac12|\le\eta\}\Big]+O(n^{-3}).
\]
The indicated conditional risks and polynomials are used only on
the central event, where both arms are nonempty for large $n$;
each integrand containing its indicator is defined as zero off
that event. The fixed-fraction and Bernoulli Taylor polynomials
need not coincide conditionally: the second identity follows by
transferring both to the capped risks, not by identifying the polynomials.
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
Conditional on $n\bar W=m$, Bernoulli assignment is uniform over
assignments with $m$ treated units, exactly as in a CRT. The law of
total expectation therefore identifies the conditional capped risk
exactly. The omitted event contributes at most
\[
c^2\Pr(|\bar W-1/2|>\eta)\le2c^2e^{-2n\eta^2}.
\]
Applying Lemma \ref{lemm:localization} to the Bernoulli risk and
uniformly to the conditional risks proves the polynomial identity
with $O(n^{-3})$ error.
\end{proof}

The proof of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} combines these lemmas, a
reduction from the CRT to the Bernoulli design, and the Bernoulli
calculation.

\begin{theo}
\label{theo:crt_reduction}
Suppose the Bernoulli design has second-order difference
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bern_second_order}
\MSEexp_\Bern(\hat{\tau}_\IREG)-\MSEexp_\Bern(\hat{\tau}_\REG)
=
\frac{D_\Bern}{n^2}+O(n^{-3})
\end{equation}
at $\pi=1/2$, and let the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} hold. Then, under
complete randomization with $n_1=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ treated units,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:crt_second_order}
\MSEexp_\CRT(\hat{\tau}_\IREG)-\MSEexp_\CRT(\hat{\tau}_\REG)
=
\frac{D_\Bern+4G}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
The same identity holds for the capped MSE.
\end{theo}

\begin{proof}
The correction comes from treatment imbalance: Bernoulli assignment
occasionally produces unequal arms, where IREG has a first-order
advantage. Averaging this advantage contributes $-4G/n^2$ to the
Bernoulli comparison. A balanced trial removes that contribution.

\emph{Step 1: average the first-order variance difference.}
Let $n_1=\sum_{i=1}^n W_i$ under the Bernoulli design and write
$r=n_1/n$. Conditional on $n_1=m$, the treatment assignment has the same distribution as a CRT with treatment fraction $r$. Fix
$\eta\in(0,\frac12)$ and write
$\mathcal A_\eta=\{|r-1/2|\le\eta\}$. All expectations of
functions containing $r^{-1}$ or $(1-r)^{-1}$ below are restricted to
$\mathcal A_\eta$; their products with the indicator are defined
as zero off that event. By Lemma \ref{lemm:bern_conditional}, $\MSEexp_\Bern$ is, up to $O(n^{-3})$, the average over $n_1$ of
$\MSEexp_{\CRT,r}$ restricted to $|r-\frac12|\le\eta$, and by Lemma \ref{lemm:conditional} the latter admits the expansion \eqref{eq:conditional_expansion} uniformly on that set, with $V_j$ and $c_j$ infinitely differentiable. At treatment fraction $r$ the first-order variance difference is, by Proposition
\ref{prop:first_order},
\[
V_{\IREG}(r)-V_{\REG}(r)
=
-\frac{(1-2r)^2}{r(1-r)}G,
\]
and
\[
\frac{(1-2r)^2}{r(1-r)}
=
16u^2+O(u^4),
\qquad
u=r-\tfrac12.
\]
Under Bernoulli randomization with $\pi=1/2$,
$\EEE[u^2]=1/(4n)$ and $\EEE[u^4]=O(n^{-2})$.
Restricting these bounded moments to $\mathcal A_\eta$ changes
them by an exponentially small amount. The event is symmetric under
$u\mapsto-u$, so $\EEE[u\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_\eta}]=0$.
It follows that
\[
\frac{1}{n}
\EEE\big[\{V_{\IREG}(r)-V_{\REG}(r)\}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_\eta}\big]
=
-\frac{4G}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\]
\emph{Step 2: average the second-order coefficient.}
For the second-order coefficients, a second-order Taylor expansion of
$c_\IREG-c_\REG$ around $1/2$ together with the restricted moment identities just established gives
\[
\EEE[\{c_{\IREG}(r)-c_{\REG}(r)\}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_\eta}]
=
c_{\IREG}(1/2)-c_{\REG}(1/2)+O(n^{-1}).
\]
Averaging \eqref{eq:conditional_expansion} over $n_1$ therefore yields
\[
\MSEexp_{\Bern}(\hat{\tau}_\IREG)
-
\MSEexp_{\Bern}(\hat{\tau}_\REG)
=
\frac{
c_{\IREG}(1/2)-c_{\REG}(1/2)-4G
}{n^2}
+O(n^{-3}),
\]
so
\[
D_{\Bern}
=
c_{\IREG}(1/2)-c_{\REG}(1/2)-4G.
\]
\emph{Step 3: fix the arm sizes.}
Under balanced complete randomization, $r=1/2$ for even $n$, giving
\[
D_{\CRT}
=
c_{\IREG}(1/2)-c_{\REG}(1/2)
=
D_{\Bern}+4G.
\]
For even $n$, Lemma \ref{lemm:conditional} therefore gives directly
\[
\MSEexp_\CRT(\hat{\tau}_\IREG)-\MSEexp_\CRT(\hat{\tau}_\REG)
=
\frac{D_{\Bern}+4G}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\]
For odd $n$, $r-1/2=O(n^{-1})$. The first-order variance difference is
quadratic in $r-1/2$, so its contribution to the MSE is $O(n^{-3})$;
the smoothness of $c_\IREG-c_\REG$ gives an $O(n^{-1})$ change in the
second-order coefficient, which is also $O(n^{-3})$ after division by
$n^2$. Thus the same expansion holds for odd $n$. Lemma \ref{lemm:localization} gives the capped-risk conclusion.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The second-order constant under Bernoulli assignment}

We now calculate $D_\Bern$. The tensor notation below is used only to
organize the contractions in the Taylor expansion; Lemma
\ref{lemm:matrix} converts the result to the matrix notation of the main
theorem. Here $F$ captures a fourth-moment contraction, $Q$ and
$\mathcal R$ capture third-moment contractions, and $A_P,B_P,C_P$
combine third moments with arm-specific residual structure.

\begin{theo}
\label{theo:bern_deficiency}
Let the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} hold, except that
$W_i\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(1/2)$ independently of $(X_i,Y_i(0),Y_i(1))$. Define
\begin{align}
T_{3,ijk} &= \EEE[Z_iZ_jZ_k],
&
T_{4,ijkl} &= \EEE[Z_iZ_jZ_kZ_l],
&
q_i &= T_{3,ijk}\delta_{jk},
&
L_{ij} &= T_{3,iab}T_{3,jab},
\end{align}
write $S=q=\EEE[Z\|Z\|^2]$, and set $\mathcal E=\EEE[ZZ^\top\varepsilon]$ and $P=\mathcal E_W=\EEE[ZZ^\top(W-\tfrac12)\varepsilon]$. Here $P$ denotes this residual-moment matrix, not the superpopulation law. For the scalar contractions
\begin{align}
G&=\|\gamma\|^2,
&
F&=\gamma_i\gamma_jT_{4,ijkk},
&
Q&=(\gamma^\top q)^2,
&
\mathcal R&=\gamma^\top L\gamma,
\\
A_P&=\gamma_iT_{3,ijk}P_{jk},
&
B_P&=(\gamma^\top q)\tr(P),
&
C_P&=\gamma^\top Pq,
\end{align}
we have
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bern_mse_diff}
\MSEexp_{\Bern}(\hat{\tau}_\IREG)
-
\MSEexp_{\Bern}(\hat{\tau}_\REG)
=
\frac{D_{\Bern}}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Dbern}
D_{\Bern}
=
-(2p+7)G
+F-Q-3\mathcal R
-16A_P-8B_P+8C_P
+8\tr(\mathcal E^2)
+8S^\top \EEE[Z\varepsilon^2].
\end{equation}
The same expansion holds for the capped MSE.
\end{theo}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: remove population main effects and whiten the covariates.}
On the neighborhood used in Lemma \ref{lemm:localization}, both REG and IREG contain the regressors $1$, $W$ and $X$ and their fits are uniquely defined.
Subtracting any linear combination of these regressors from the outcome changes the fitted coefficient on $W$ by the corresponding amount while leaving the estimation error unchanged, so we may subtract the population main effects and the term $\tau W_i$ and work with the exact representation
\[
Y_i
=
W_i X_i^\top\{\beta^*_{(1)}-\beta^*_{(0)}\}
+\varepsilon_i.
\]
This local invariance determines the Taylor coefficients; any failure of equivariance in the completion affects capped risk by only $O(n^{-3})$. Whitening as in \eqref{eq:whitening} gives the canonical  representation
$Y_i=W_i\gamma^\top Z_i+\varepsilon_i$, whose population ATE is
zero. In the following calculation, $Y_i$ denotes this transformed
outcome, $g(\mu)=0$, and the resulting Taylor errors equal those
for the original estimators about their original target $\tau$.
The raw-moment transformation is affine, so the Taylor-polynomial
risks in Definition \ref{def:msexp} are preserved. We write
\[
W_i=\tfrac12+\omega_i,
\qquad
\omega_i\in\{-\tfrac12,\tfrac12\},
\qquad
\EEE[\omega_i]=0,
\qquad
\omega_i^2=\tfrac14.
\]

\emph{Step 2: identify the three second-order contributions.}
Use the Bernoulli raw-moment vector $\mathcal V_i$ in
Definition \ref{def:msexp}, formed with the transformed outcome
and whitened covariates from Step 1, and the corresponding map
$g_j$. Suppress the estimator subscript temporarily, so that
$\htau=g(\bar{\mathcal V})$ locally and
$\tilde\tau_n$ is given by \eqref{eq:hall_expansion}. Let
\[
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{ij}
=
\Cov(\mathcal V_{1,i},\mathcal V_{1,j}),
\qquad
\mu^{\mathcal V}_{3,ijk}
=
\EEE[
(\mathcal V_{1,i}-\mu_i)
(\mathcal V_{1,j}-\mu_j)
(\mathcal V_{1,k}-\mu_k)
]
\]
be the covariance and third central-moment tensors of one unit's
raw-moment vector; the first subscript in $\mathcal V_{1,i}$ denotes
the unit and the second denotes its coordinate. In the next three
contractions all repeated indices range over raw-moment coordinates.
Expanding the square of \eqref{eq:hall_expansion} gives
\[
n\MSEexp_\Bern(\htau_j)
=V^*+\frac{\mathcal T_{1,j}+\mathcal T_{2,j}+\mathcal T_{3,j}}{n}
+O(n^{-2}),
\]
where, suppressing $j$ again,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hall_T1}
\mathcal T_1
=
a_iH_{jk}\mu^{\mathcal V}_{3,ijk},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hall_T2_bern}
\mathcal T_2
=
\frac14 H_{ij}H_{kl}
\p{
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{ik}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{jl}
+
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{il}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{jk}
+
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{ij}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{kl}
},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hall_T3_bern}
\mathcal T_3
=
\frac13 a_iA_{3,jkl}\p{
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{ij}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{kl}
+
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{ik}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{jl}
+
\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{il}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{jk}
},
\end{equation}
The omitted terms are $O(n^{-2})$ in $n\MSEexp$:
the non-pairing part of the fourth moment and all degree-five and
degree-six products in the squared polynomial contribute $O(n^{-3})$
to $\MSEexp$, by the same centered sample-mean moment count as in
Lemma \ref{lemm:conditional}. Lemma \ref{lemm:localization} gives the same $O(n^{-3})$ remainder for the capped MSE. The difference between the order-$n^{-2}$ MSE
coefficients of REG and IREG is obtained by evaluating these three
contractions for the two estimators.

The three contributions have a simple origin: $\mathcal T_1$ is the
product of the linear and quadratic Taylor terms, $\mathcal T_2$ is
the square of the quadratic term, and $\mathcal T_3$ is the product of
the linear and cubic terms. Thus the calculation requires derivatives
only through order three.

\emph{Step 3: compute derivatives from the least-squares normal equations.}
For REG, order the regressors as $(1,Z,W)$; for IREG, use
$(1,Z,W,WZ)$. In these orders, the population Gram matrices and their
inverses in the whitened coordinates are
\[
A_R=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&1/2\\
0&I_p&0\\
1/2&0&1/2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A_R^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0&-2\\
0&I_p&0\\
-2&0&4
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A_I=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&1/2&0\\
0&I_p&0&I_p/2\\
1/2&0&1/2&0\\
0&I_p/2&0&I_p/2
\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
A_I^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0&-2&0\\
0&2I_p&0&-2I_p\\
-2&0&4&0\\
0&-2I_p&0&4I_p
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\theta_R=(0,\gamma/2,0),
\qquad
\theta_I=(0,0,0,\gamma).
\]

For either regressor vector, let $A(v)$ and $b(v)$ be its Gram
matrix and regressor--outcome cross-moment vector as functions
of the raw moments $v$, and write $A=A(\mu)$ and $b=b(\mu)$.
These functions are affine in the chosen raw-moment coordinates,
so their derivatives of order two and higher vanish.
For a perturbation direction $h_s$ of the raw moments, write $dA_s$
and $db_s$ for the corresponding perturbations of $A$ and $b$.
Writing $\Omega=A^{-1}$ and $\theta=\Omega b$, differentiation of
$A\theta=b$ gives the exact derivative identities
\[
D\theta[h]
=
\Omega(db-dA\,\theta),
\]
\[
D^2\theta[h_1,h_2]
=
-\Omega dA_1\Omega(db_2-dA_2\theta)
-\Omega dA_2\Omega(db_1-dA_1\theta),
\]
\[
D^3\theta[h_1,h_2,h_3]
=
\sum_{\varsigma\in\mathfrak S_3}
\Omega dA_{\varsigma_1}\Omega dA_{\varsigma_2}
\Omega(db_{\varsigma_3}-dA_{\varsigma_3}\theta).
\]
Here $\mathfrak S_3$ is the set of all six permutations
$(\varsigma_1,\varsigma_2,\varsigma_3)$ of $(1,2,3)$;
$dA$ and $db$ in the first identity are the perturbations in direction $h$.

\emph{Step 4: account for the centering in IREG.}
REG's estimator is the treatment coefficient,
$g_\REG(v)=\theta_W(v)$. IREG's is
$g_\IREG(v)=\theta_W(v)+\bar z(v)^\top\theta_{WZ}(v)$, where
$\bar z(v)$ is the full-sample covariate mean, a linear coordinate of
$v$ that vanishes at $\mu$ in whitened coordinates, and $\theta_{WZ}=\gamma$ at $\mu$. The product rule gives
\[
a_\IREG[h]
=
D\theta_W[h]+D\bar z[h]^\top\gamma,
\]
\[
H_\IREG[h_1,h_2]
=
D^2\theta_W[h_1,h_2]
+
D\bar z[h_1]^\top D\theta_{WZ}[h_2]
+
D\bar z[h_2]^\top D\theta_{WZ}[h_1],
\]
\[
A_{3,\IREG}[h_1,h_2,h_3]
=
D^3\theta_W[h_1,h_2,h_3]
+
\sum_{\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}}
D\bar z[h_i]^\top D^2\theta_{WZ}[h_j,h_k],
\]
where the sum means the three terms with
$(i,j,k)=(1,2,3),(2,1,3),(3,1,2)$. The covariance and
third-moment blocks of $\mathcal V_i$ are products of moments of
$(Z_i,\omega_i,\varepsilon_i)$ under the canonical representation in
Step 1, with $W_i$ marginally Bernoulli$(1/2)$.

\emph{Step 5: evaluate the contractions and take IREG minus REG.}
Write $\Delta\mathcal T_k=\mathcal T_{k,\IREG}-\mathcal T_{k,\REG}$.
Substituting these derivative tensors and the covariance and third-moment blocks of $\mathcal V_i$ into \eqref{eq:hall_T1}--\eqref{eq:hall_T3_bern}, we reduce the resulting indexed expressions using tensor symmetries, dummy-index relabeling, and the identities implied by $\EEE[ZZ^\top]=I_p$ and $\omega_i^2=1/4$. This gives
\begin{align}
\Delta\mathcal T_1
&=
2G+2F+8\Xi_\gamma,
\label{eq:dT1}
\\
\Delta\mathcal T_2
&=
-3G-F-Q-\mathcal R-8\Xi_\gamma-8A_P-8B_P,
\label{eq:dT2}
\\
\Delta\mathcal T_3
&=
-(2p+6)G-2\mathcal R-8A_P+8C_P
+8\tr(\mathcal E^2)
+8S^\top \EEE[Z\varepsilon^2],
\label{eq:dT3}
\end{align}
where
\[
\Xi_\gamma
=
\EEE[(\gamma^\top Z)\|Z\|^2\omega\varepsilon]
\]
with $\omega=W-\frac12$. The accompanying verification script
enumerates the full Cartesian products of the expansion directions and
reduces the resulting indexed expressions using only tensor symmetries, dummy-index relabeling, and the identities implied by
$\EEE[ZZ^\top]=I_p$ and $\omega_i^2=1/4$, with the dimension $p$ kept
symbolic. The symbolic remainder in each of the three rows is
identically zero; the verification is implemented in
\texttt{verification/general\char`\_p\char`\_symbolic\char`\_certificate.py} in the replication repository and runs in under a minute.

\emph{Step 6: collect the terms.}
Adding the three rows gives the coefficient of $G$ as
$2-3-(2p+6)=-(2p+7)$ and the coefficient of $F$ as $2-1=1$.
The $\Xi_\gamma$ terms cancel. The remaining coefficients are $-1$
for $Q$, $-3$ for $\mathcal R$, and $(-16,-8,8)$ for
$(A_P,B_P,C_P)$, together with the two residual terms in
\eqref{eq:Dbern}. This proves the stated formula.

One contraction can be checked directly without the full tensor
calculation. The third covariance pairing in
\eqref{eq:hall_T2_bern} is
\[
\frac14(H_{ij}\Sigma^{\mathcal V}_{ij})^2.
\]
It is the square of the leading bias coefficient. By the bias
calculation in Lemma \ref{lemm:bias}, those coefficients at balanced
allocation are $b_I=-4\tr(P)$ and
$b_R=-4\tr(P)-\gamma^\top q$. They are also the Bernoulli
coefficients: the conditional bias coefficient is smooth in $r$,
and Lemma \ref{lemm:localization}, applied to clipped errors, changes the mean by only $O(n^{-2})$. Conditioning on the treatment count and using its symmetry then preserves the leading bias coefficient. Thus this pairing contributes
\[
b_I^2-b_R^2
=-(\gamma^\top q)^2
-8(\gamma^\top q)\tr(P)
=-Q-8B_P.
\]
These are exactly the two bias-squared terms in the second row.
The other pairings and the linear--cubic contraction account for the
remaining variance contributions.

The computational certificate checks the remaining algebra. Derived
by hand and displayed above: the expansion terms \eqref{eq:hall_T1}--\eqref{eq:hall_T3_bern}, the Gram matrices and coefficients at $\mu$, the derivative identities, the product-rule structure of the IREG centering functional, and the reduction rules. Verified symbolically by the accompanying implementation: the substitution of these objects into \eqref{eq:hall_T1}--\eqref{eq:hall_T3_bern} and the reduction of the resulting indexed sums to the rows \eqref{eq:dT1}--\eqref{eq:dT3}, with symbolic $p$ and zero remainder. The resulting constant is checked independently by Monte Carlo in Appendix
\ref{sec:numerical-verification}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Recovering the matrix formula in the main theorem}

Adding the $4G$ correction of Theorem \ref{theo:crt_reduction} to
the Bernoulli constant in Theorem \ref{theo:bern_deficiency} changes
only the coefficient of $G$, from $-(2p+7)$ to $-(2p+3)$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unsimp}
D_p
=
-(2p+3)G
+F-Q-3\mathcal R
-16A_P-8B_P+8C_P
+8\tr(\mathcal E^2)
+8S^\top \EEE[Z\varepsilon^2],
\end{equation}
which Lemma \ref{lemm:matrix} rewrites in the matrix form of
Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}. Together with Lemma \ref{lemm:localization}, this proves its capped-MSE identity.

\begin{lemm}[Equivalent matrix form]
\label{lemm:matrix}
With $K$, $S$, $S_\gamma$, $\mathcal E$ and $\mathcal E_W$ as in
Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency},
\[
G=\|\gamma\|^2,
\qquad
F=\gamma^\top K\gamma,
\qquad
Q=(\gamma^\top S)^2,
\qquad
\mathcal R=\|S_\gamma\|_F^2,
\]
\[
A_P=\tr(S_\gamma \mathcal E_W),
\qquad
B_P=(\gamma^\top S)\tr(\mathcal E_W),
\qquad
C_P=\gamma^\top \mathcal E_WS.
\]
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
First,
\[
F
=
\gamma_i\gamma_jT_{4,ijkl}\delta_{kl}
=
\gamma^\top \EEE[ZZ^\top\|Z\|^2]\gamma
=
\gamma^\top K\gamma,
\]
and
\[
S=\EEE[Z_iZ_jZ_j]_{i=1}^p=q
\]
gives
\[
Q=(\gamma^\top q)^2=(\gamma^\top S)^2.
\]
Next,
\[
(S_\gamma)_{ab}
=
\EEE[Z_aZ_b(Z^\top\gamma)]
=
T_{3,abk}\gamma_k,
\]
so
\[
\|S_\gamma\|_F^2
=
\sum_{a,b}(S_\gamma)_{ab}^2
=
\gamma_k\gamma_\ell T_{3,abk}T_{3,ab\ell}
=
\gamma_k\gamma_\ell L_{k\ell}
=
\mathcal R.
\]
Since $S_\gamma$ and $\mathcal E_W$ are symmetric,
\[
\tr(S_\gamma \mathcal E_W)
=
(S_\gamma)_{jk}(\mathcal E_W)_{jk}
=
\gamma_iT_{3,ijk}P_{jk}
=
A_P;
\]
finally,
\[
B_P
=
(\gamma^\top q)\tr(P)
=
(\gamma^\top S)\tr(\mathcal E_W)
\]
and
\[
C_P
=
\gamma^\top Pq
=
\gamma^\top \mathcal E_WS.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{An exact estimator difference and the leading biases}

The next identity explains both the first-order equivalence of REG
and IREG and the control variate used in the numerical verification.
Their difference is a product of covariate imbalance, scatter-matrix
imbalance, and the difference between the fitted arm slopes.

We begin with an exact identity used by the variance-reduced
verification of Appendix \ref{sec:numerical-verification}.

\begin{lemm}[Exact difference identity]
\label{lemm:exact_diff}
Let $\bar X_w$ and
\[
\mathcal S_w
=
\sum_{i:W_i=w}
(X_i-\bar X_w)(X_i-\bar X_w)^\top
\]
denote the within-arm means and scatter matrices, let
\[
\hbeta_w
=
\mathcal S_w^{-1}
\sum_{i:W_i=w}
(X_i-\bar X_w)(Y_i-\bar Y_w),
\]
and write $d_X=\bar X_1-\bar X_0$. Then, whenever
$\mathcal S_0$, $\mathcal S_1$ and $\mathcal S_0+\mathcal S_1$ are
invertible and $n_1=n_0$,
\[
\htau_\REG
=
\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0
-
d_X^\top
(\mathcal S_1+\mathcal S_0)^{-1}
(\mathcal S_1\hbeta_1+\mathcal S_0\hbeta_0),
\]
\[
\htau_\IREG
=
\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0
-
d_X^\top
\frac{\hbeta_1+\hbeta_0}{2},
\]
and consequently
\[
\htau_\IREG-\htau_\REG
=
\tfrac12\,d_X^\top
(\mathcal S_1+\mathcal S_0)^{-1}
(\mathcal S_1-\mathcal S_0)
(\hbeta_1-\hbeta_0)
=O_P(n^{-1}).
\]
Here the algebraic identity is exact on the stated invertibility
event; the stochastic order uses the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}.
The probability of invertibility tends to one, since the normalized
within-arm scatter matrices converge to $\Sigma\succ0$.
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
Both expressions for the estimators are the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell
representations of \eqref{eq:estimators}: the coefficient on $X$ in the REG regression is the pooled within-arm slope
$(\mathcal S_1+\mathcal S_0)^{-1}
(\mathcal S_1\hbeta_1+\mathcal S_0\hbeta_0)$, and IREG fits the two arms separately, so with $n_1=n_0$ the overall covariate mean satisfies
\[
\bar X_1-\bar X
=
d_X/2
=
-(\bar X_0-\bar X).
\]
Subtracting,
\[
\htau_\IREG-\htau_\REG
=
d_X^\top
\left[
(\mathcal S_1+\mathcal S_0)^{-1}
(\mathcal S_1\hbeta_1+\mathcal S_0\hbeta_0)
-
\tfrac12(\hbeta_1+\hbeta_0)
\right]
\]
\[
=
\tfrac12d_X^\top
(\mathcal S_1+\mathcal S_0)^{-1}
(\mathcal S_1-\mathcal S_0)
(\hbeta_1-\hbeta_0).
\]
The stochastic order follows from
\[
d_X=O_P(n^{-1/2}),
\qquad
(\mathcal S_1+\mathcal S_0)^{-1}
(\mathcal S_1-\mathcal S_0)=O_P(n^{-1/2}),
\qquad
\hbeta_1-\hbeta_0=O_P(1).
\]
\end{proof}

In whitened coordinates, the same Taylor expansion gives
\[
\htau_\IREG-\htau_\REG
=\tfrac14d_Z^\top(A_1-A_0)\gamma+O_P(n^{-3/2}),
\]
where $d_Z=\bar Z_1-\bar Z_0$ and $A_w$ is the arm-$w$ mean
of $ZZ^\top-I_p$; the arm sizes here remain $n_1=n_0=n/2$.
Indeed, replacing each centered scatter matrix by its uncentered
second-moment version changes its normalized value by $O_P(n^{-1})$;
the inverse-Gram and slope expansions each contribute another
$O_P(n^{-1/2})$ correction to their leading terms. Multiplication
by $d_Z=O_P(n^{-1/2})$ gives the stated remainder.
The leading quadratic term is used in the control variate of
\eqref{eq:cv_identity} below.

The final lemma separates the leading biases of the two estimators.
Both share the arm-specific residual contribution; REG has the
additional contribution $-\gamma^\top S/n$ from the interaction of
covariate skewness and treatment-effect heterogeneity.

\begin{lemm}[Leading clipped-error bias]
\label{lemm:bias}
Under the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} and balanced complete randomization, for $e_{j,c}$ as in Remark \ref{rema:bias},
\[
\EEE[e_{\IREG,c}]
=
-\frac{4\tr(\mathcal E_W)}{n}+O(n^{-2}),
\]
\[
\EEE[e_{\REG,c}]
=
-\frac{4\tr(\mathcal E_W)+\gamma^\top S}{n}+O(n^{-2}).
\]
\end{lemm}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma \ref{lemm:localization}, it suffices to calculate the cubic Taylor-polynomial biases up to $O(n^{-2})$; no expectation of an inverse sample Gram matrix is required. First suppose
$n$ is even and set $m=n/2$. In whitened coordinates, define
\[
d_Z=\bar Z_1-\bar Z_0,\qquad
t_w=\frac1m\sum_{i:W_i=w}Z_i\varepsilon_i(w),\qquad
A_w=\frac1m\sum_{i:W_i=w}(Z_iZ_i^\top-I_p).
\]
Each is a centered sample-mean quantity. Write
$b_w=\Sigma^{1/2}\beta^*_{(w)}$ for the population arm slope
in whitened coordinates, so $b_1-b_0=\gamma$.
The common linear error term is
\[
L=\gamma^\top\bar Z+\bar\varepsilon_1-\bar\varepsilon_0,
\qquad \EEE[L]=0.
\]
Here $\bar Z=(\bar Z_1+\bar Z_0)/2$ and
$\bar\varepsilon_w=m^{-1}\sum_{i:W_i=w}\varepsilon_i(w)$.

\emph{Step 1: identify the quadratic terms.}
The linear Taylor term of an arm slope is $t_w$. The linear Taylor
term of the pooled slope, expanded around $(b_1+b_0)/2$, is
\[
\frac{t_1+t_0}{2}+\frac14(A_1-A_0)\gamma.
\]
To verify the latter expression, expand the pooled normal equations:
the first perturbation of the within-arm Gram matrix is
$(A_1+A_0)/2$, whereas its cross-moment perturbation includes
$(A_1b_1+A_0b_0)/2+(t_1+t_0)/2$.
Subtracting the Gram perturbation times $(b_1+b_0)/2$ leaves the
displayed expression. Products of arm means enter only at degree two
in the slope and hence at degree three in the treatment estimator.

The quadratic estimator terms are therefore
\[
q_{2,I}=-\frac12d_Z^\top(t_1+t_0),
\qquad
q_{2,R}=-d_Z^\top\left\{
\frac{t_1+t_0}{2}+\frac14(A_1-A_0)\gamma\right\}.
\]
The cubic Taylor terms have expectations $O(n^{-2})$, because a
product of three centered sample means has expectation of that order.
Thus the leading expansion biases equal $\EEE[q_{2,I}]$ and
$\EEE[q_{2,R}]$, respectively, with remainder $O(n^{-2})$.

\emph{Step 2: evaluate the residual contribution.}
Independence of the arms and centering give the exact identities
\[
\EEE[d_Z^\top t_1]
=\frac1m\EEE[\|Z\|^2\varepsilon(1)],
\qquad
\EEE[d_Z^\top t_0]
=-\frac1m\EEE[\|Z\|^2\varepsilon(0)].
\]
Consequently,
\[
\EEE[q_{2,I}]
=-\frac{1}{2m}\tr\left\{
\EEE[ZZ^\top\varepsilon(1)]
-\EEE[ZZ^\top\varepsilon(0)]\right\}
=-\frac{4\tr(\mathcal E_W)}{n}.
\]

\emph{Step 3: evaluate REG's additional heterogeneity contribution.}
For either arm,
\[
\EEE[\bar Z_w^\top A_w\gamma]
=\frac1m\EEE[Z^\top(ZZ^\top-I_p)\gamma]
=\frac{\gamma^\top S}{m}.
\]
The cross-arm terms vanish, so
\[
\EEE[d_Z^\top(A_1-A_0)\gamma]
=\frac{2\gamma^\top S}{m}.
\]
Multiplication by $-1/4$ adds $-\gamma^\top S/n$ to REG's
bias, proving both formulas for even $n$.

For odd $n$, the arm fraction differs from $1/2$ by $O(n^{-1})$.
The leading quadratic expectation is $n^{-1}$ times a smooth
function of that fraction, by the same moment calculation as in
Lemma \ref{lemm:conditional}. Its change is therefore $O(n^{-2})$.
The formulas hold for odd $n$ with the same stated remainder.
\end{proof}

\section{Numerical illustration of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}}
\label{sec:numerical-verification}

The symbolic certificate verifies the algebra of Theorem
\ref{theo:bern_deficiency} exactly. Simulation serves a different
purpose: it assesses whether the fitted estimators approach the
predicted constant in the chosen designs and whether the experiment
can distinguish that constant from plausible alternatives, including
an omitted CRT correction. These checks provide numerical evidence;
they do not replace the proof. The simulations below use ordinary, uncapped squared errors, whereas Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} concerns capped MSE. Additional tail control is needed to transfer the expansion to ordinary MSE, as discussed in Appendix \ref{sec:proofs}.

\paragraph{Exact targets.}
We use data-generating processes with whitened covariates having
independent coordinates with known moments. The oracle residuals take
the form
\[
\varepsilon(w)=g_w(Z)+s_w(Z)\eta,
\]
where $\eta$ is independent of $Z$ with mean zero and unit variance,
$g_w$ is a polynomial orthogonal to $(1,Z)$, and $s_w^2$ is a polynomial. Each term in $D_p$ is therefore a finite combination of population moments and can be computed in exact rational arithmetic. The code also checks $\EEE[g_w]=\EEE[Zg_w]=0$, so that violations of the orthogonality conditions cannot pass unnoticed.

We use six designs, chosen so that every nontrivial block of $D_p$ is
active in at least one design: Gaussian homoskedastic covariates;
centered-exponential covariates with heterogeneity; centered-exponential covariates with heteroskedasticity and $\gamma=0$; centered-exponential covariates with heterogeneity, arm-specific misspecification, and arm-specific heteroskedasticity; Gaussian covariates with arm-specific misspecification and heteroskedasticity; and a mixed skewed/Gaussian design. These designs are analytically tractable and differ from the inference designs of Section \ref{sec:feasible}; their exact definitions are in \texttt{simulations/dgps.py} of the replication repository.

\paragraph{Variance reduction for numerical verification.}
Our simulation estimates the finite-sample quantity
\[
D_n
=
n^2\{\MSE(\htau_\IREG)-\MSE(\htau_\REG)\}.
\]
Under the additional mean-square remainder conditions discussed in
Appendix \ref{sec:proofs}, $D_n$ converges to the expansion-MSE
constant $D_p$. Comparing the simulated $D_n$ with $D_p$ therefore
checks how closely the fitted estimators follow the second-order
prediction in the chosen designs.

Suppose we generate $B$ independent datasets, each with even sample
size $n$ and $n/2$ units per arm. On replication $b$, let
$e_{j,b}=\htau_{j,b}-\tau$ and define
\[
T_b=n^2(e_{\IREG,b}^2-e_{\REG,b}^2).
\]
When the fitted estimators are defined and have finite second moments,
the average of the $T_b$ is unbiased for $D_n$. However, individual
values of $T_b$ can fluctuate substantially, making this direct
Monte Carlo estimate noisy.

To identify the leading source of this noise, write
$d_Z=\bar Z_1-\bar Z_0$, let $A_w$ be the arm-$w$ mean of
$ZZ^\top-I_p$, and let $\bar\varepsilon_w$ be the arm-$w$ mean
of the oracle residuals. Define
\[
d_1=\tfrac14d_Z^\top(A_1-A_0)\gamma,
\qquad
L=\gamma^\top\bar Z+\bar\varepsilon_1-\bar\varepsilon_0.
\]
By Lemma \ref{lemm:exact_diff}, $d_1$ is the leading term of
$\htau_\IREG-\htau_\REG$, while $L$ is the common first-order
estimation error. Thus
\[
\begin{aligned}
e_\IREG^2-e_\REG^2
&=(e_\IREG-e_\REG)(e_\IREG+e_\REG)\\
&=2d_1L+O_P(n^{-2}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $d_1=O_P(n^{-1})$ and $L=O_P(n^{-1/2})$, the term
$2n^2d_1L$ is $O_P(\sqrt n)$. Under the sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}, its variance is $O(n)$. This term can therefore
produce large fluctuations in $T_b$, even though the quantity we
want to estimate remains bounded as $n$ grows.

We reduce this noise using a control variate. On each replication,
set
\[
C_b=2n^2d_{1,b}L_b.
\]
The quantity $C_b$ captures the leading fluctuation in $T_b$.
Subtracting its centered version gives the adjusted estimate
\[
\hat D_p
=
\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B
\big\{T_b-(C_b-\EEE[C_b])\big\}.
\]
Because $\EEE[C_b-\EEE[C_b]]=0$, this adjustment leaves the
expectation unchanged: $\EEE[\hat D_p]=D_n$. At a fixed $n$,
$\hat D_p$ estimates the scaled finite-sample MSE difference,
rather than being unbiased for the limiting constant $D_p$.

The control mean is available analytically because the simulation
distribution is known. For an arm of size $m=n/2$, the expectation
of a product of three centered IID sample means equals the
corresponding one-unit third joint cumulant divided by $m^2$.
Applying this identity gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cv_identity}
\EEE[d_1L]
=
\frac{1}{n^2}
\EEE\Big[
(\|Z\|^2-1)(\gamma^\top Z)
\{\gamma^\top Z+\varepsilon(1)-\varepsilon(0)\}
\Big],
\end{equation}
and hence $\EEE[C_b]=2n^2\EEE[d_1L]$. Only this control mean is
computed analytically; the remaining contribution is estimated
from the simulated datasets.

The adjustment removes the leading fluctuation in probability:
\[
T_b-C_b
=
n^2\{e_{\IREG,b}^2-e_{\REG,b}^2-2d_{1,b}L_b\}
=
O_P(1).
\]
This stochastic-order statement alone does not guarantee bounded
variance. A sufficient additional condition is
\[
\sup_n\EEE[(T_b-C_b)^2]<\infty.
\]
Under this condition, the adjusted per-replication variance is
$O(1)$, and the Monte Carlo standard error of $\hat D_p$ is
$O(B^{-1/2})$ uniformly in $n$. The sampling and moment conditions of Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency}
alone do not establish this condition, because the fitted
estimators involve inverse sample Gram matrices. The standard
errors reported below are empirical simulation summaries.

The replication code reports both the raw and adjusted estimates.
In the Gaussian design, the raw per-replication standard deviation
increases from $151$ at $n=200$ to $293$ at $n=800$, while the
control-variate standard deviation is $56$ and $55$, respectively.
At $n=3200$, the control-variate Monte Carlo standard error is
ten times smaller than that of the raw statistic.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table \ref{tab:validation} reports the exact targets and the
control-variate estimates at $n\in\{200,800,3200\}$, using
$B=400{,}000$ replications for the two smaller sample sizes and
$B=120{,}000$ for $n=3200$. In these simulations, the control variate substantially reduces the
observed per-replication standard deviation, making the estimated
Monte Carlo errors small enough to assess finite-$n$ discrepancies.

The largest discrepancy occurs in the all-blocks design (fourth row).
There the estimate is $32.40$ at $n=200$, compared with the target
$44.52$, a difference of $12.1$ (about $22$ standard errors). The

The observed increments do not establish a slower asymptotic rate: these sample sizes may be outside that regime, and the simulations use uncapped MSE. We do not extrapolate. The ablation in Table

\paragraph{Power: what the verification would reject.}
Agreement with $D_p$ is more informative when the simulation can also
detect errors in the formula. We therefore compare the estimates with
several deliberately perturbed candidate constants. Table \ref{tab:ablation} reports
$z$-statistics at $n=3200$ for the exact $D_p$ and several perturbed
formulas: the Bernoulli constant $D_\Bern=D_p-4G$, which omits the CRT
correction; the Gaussian special case $-(p+1)G$; and versions of $D_p$
with individual blocks removed.

The exact formula has $|z|\leq2.6$ in every design, whereas each
alternative has $|z|>3$ in at least one design, in some cases by tens of standard errors. The check has limited power when an alternative differs from $D_p$ by less than the residual finite-$n$ bias. In the all-blocks design, for example, $D_\Bern=D_p-3.2$ is not separated from $D_p$ at $n=3200$, which is why multiple designs are useful.

Without the control variate, the standard errors at $n=3200$ would be
$1.5$ to $3.8$, four to ten times larger in the heterogeneous designs.
The Bernoulli constant would then not be separated from $D_p$ in any
design ($|z|\leq3$ throughout), so the simulation would have little
power to detect an omitted CRT correction.

\paragraph{Further covariate families and dimensions.}
The six designs above have $p\le3$ and independent coordinates. We also consider four covariate families at $p\in\{2,5,8\}$, including dependent coordinates (Table \ref{tab:families}).

For Gaussian covariates,
$D_p=-(p+1)G$. For Rademacher covariates,
$K=pI_p$ and $D_p=-(p+3)G$. For a symmetric scale mixture of normals,
where $2\%$ of units have six times the standard deviation, write
$s$ for the mixing scale ($s=6$ with probability $0.02$ and
$s=1$ otherwise). After standardizing the mixture,
\[
K=(p+2)\tilde\kappa I_p,
\qquad
\tilde\kappa=\frac{\EEE[s^4]}{\EEE[s^2]^2},
\]
and
\[
D_p
=
G\{(p+2)\tilde\kappa-(2p+3)\}.
\]
For Gaussian covariates with quadratic misspecification
$c(X^\top X-\tr\Sigma)$, where $c=0.3$ and
$\Sigma_{jk}=0.3^{|j-k|}$,
\[
\mathcal E=2c\Sigma,
\qquad
D_p=-(p+1)G+32c^2\tr(\Sigma^2).
\]

Every family uses homoskedastic $N(0,1)$ noise shared by the potential
outcomes, with $G=0.49$ ($1.96$ for the Rademacher family). Because both estimators are invariant to invertible linear transformations of the covariates, we simulate whitened covariates; dependence enters through $\gamma$ and, for the misspecified family, through $\tr(\Sigma^2)$.

Using exact targets avoids this additional Monte Carlo error.

With $n=\gz{fam:n}$ and $B=\gz{fam:B}$ replications per design, every
estimate is within \gz{fam:maxabsz} standard errors of its target, and
the control variate reduces the standard errors by factors of
\gz{fam:seratio:min} to \gz{fam:seratio:max} relative to the raw estimator
on the same replications.

\paragraph{The CRT correction and the bias decomposition.}
We check Theorem \ref{theo:crt_reduction} separately by estimating the
$n^{-2}$ constant under both balanced CRT and Bernoulli$(1/2)$ assignment on the same designs (\texttt{verification/crt\char`\_correction\char`\_certificate.py}).
In the Gaussian design, the estimated gap between the two designs is
$4.3\pm0.6$ at $n=200$ and $6.3\pm1.6$ at $n=800$, compared with the
predicted $4G=5$. In the exponential-covariate design, the corresponding gaps are $3.0\pm1.1$ and $8.6\pm3.0$. All four comparisons have $|z|<2$.

This check uses the raw statistic because the control variate is derived for the two-independent-samples representation of the CRT, so its power is limited. The ablation in Table \ref{tab:ablation} provides a sharper test: there, the Bernoulli constant is rejected by as many as thirty standard errors.

We also compare the leading coefficients in Remark \ref{rema:bias} with simulations of the ordinary, unclipped biases of both estimators
(\texttt{simulations/run\char`\_bias.py}). With $10^6$ replications, at $n=800$ all six estimated biases (REG and IREG in three designs) are within $0.8$ Monte Carlo standard errors of their leading-order
predictions. At $n=200$, the all-blocks design differs by about $11\%$
of the predicted REG bias ($z=9.3$) and by $5\%$ of the predicted IREG
bias ($z=3.5$); by $n=800$, both differences are negligible at the
reported Monte Carlo precision ($z=0.4$ and $z=-0.5$), consistent with the leading bias coefficients. Lemma \ref{lemm:bias} proves the $O(n^{-2})$ remainder for clipped errors; the ordinary-bias simulation does not establish that bound for unclipped errors.

\section{Range of validity of the second-order approximation}
\label{sec:validity}

Theorem \ref{theo:deficiency} is an asymptotic result. A separate question is when the approximation
\[
1+\Delta/n
\]
is accurate for the finite-sample MSE ratio. We examine this in the
scalar lognormal family of the introduction, with $\sigma(X)=1+X$, now with a treatment-effect slope of $0.4$ fixed while $X\sim\mathrm{LogNormal}(0,s^2)$ varies. The ratio compares IREG with REG, so values above one favor REG.
As $s$ increases, the covariate tails become heavier and the higher
moments entering $\Delta$ grow rapidly. We ask whether those
population moments describe the MSE comparison accurately at the
sample sizes used in the simulations.

We do not interpret this as a universal threshold: the point is that a large deficiency can coexist with substantial finite-sample error in the second-order approximation.

The heavy-tailed example illustrates the
same phenomenon more sharply. With $s=1.8$, $\sigma(X)=1+1.5X$, and
treatment-effect slope $0.3$, we have $\Delta\approx 3.9\times10^4$. The second-order prediction is then $78.5$, $20.4$, and $5.8$ at $n=500$, $2{,}000$, and $8{,}000$, compared with simulated MSE ratios of $1.68$, $1.34$, and $1.19$. The sign of the comparison is correct, but $1+\Delta/n$ is no longer a useful numerical approximation to the MSE ratio. In the same design, $R_\tau^2\approx0.009$, and the simulated coverage of IREG intervals without the plug-in correction is $85.6\%$ with HC1, $88.9\%$ with HC2, and $94.0\%$ with HC3, compared with $94.4\%$ for REG+HC1 and $96.7\%$ for REG+HC3; with the plug-in, IREG+HC1 covers $\gz{hte:ba:heavy_ex:IREG_HC1c:cov}\%$ (Table \ref{tab:battery}).

\paragraph{Tail sensitivity.}
The deficiency is a population-moment calculation. If extreme values
account for much of those moments, the asymptotic constant can be large
even when samples of the sizes considered seldom reach that part of
the distribution. The largest discrepancies occur in precisely these
tail-dominated designs. Table \ref{tab:tail} recomputes
$1+\Delta/n$ at $n=500$ after truncating the lognormal law at several
high quantiles; the truncated moments are available in closed form.
Truncating at the $1-10^{-4}$ quantile reduces $\Delta$ by one to three orders of magnitude, showing how strongly the deficiency depends on extreme covariate values. Even truncation at the $1-10^{-8}$ quantile still gives predictions several times larger than the simulated ratios.

In these designs, the problem begins already at first order. For the
same family without treatment-effect heterogeneity ($\gamma=0$, the
introductory design at other values of $s$), at $s=0.6$ the simulated standard deviation of $\htau_\REG$ is close to the first-order benchmark $\sqrt{V^*/n}$,
$\gz{samp:s0.6:sdR}$ versus $\gz{samp:s0.6:sdtheory}$. At $s=1.5$ and
$s=2$, the corresponding values are $\gz{samp:s1.5:sdR}$ versus $\gz{samp:s1.5:sdtheory}$ and $\gz{samp:s2.0:sdR}$ versus $\gz{samp:s2.0:sdtheory}$, respectively. Thus the heavy-tailed designs are outside the regime where the perturbative expansion gives an accurate quantitative approximation. Monte Carlo error is a secondary issue in the heaviest rows, where rare draws contribute substantially to the simulated MSE. The sign of the REG--IREG comparison agrees with the second-order calculation in every
design.

\section{Designs of the inference simulations}
\label{sec:designs}

This section specifies the data-generating processes used for the
inference comparisons. These are separate from the analytically
tractable verification designs of Appendix
\ref{sec:numerical-verification}. Each comparison uses shared
simulated datasets so that differences across interval procedures
reflect the procedure rather than a different simulation draw.

The designs of Tables \ref{tab:battery} and \ref{tab:plugin-cal} all have $n=500$, balanced complete randomization and \gz{hte:reps} replications, and every interval is computed on the same simulated datasets. The scalar lognormal designs extend the family of the introduction:
\[
\begin{gathered}
p=1,\qquad
X_i\sim\mathrm{LogNormal}(0,s^2),\qquad
u_i\sim N(0,1),\\
Y_i(0)=0.9X_i+\sigma(X_i)u_i,\qquad
Y_i(1)=Y_i(0)+1+gX_i,
\end{gathered}
\]
with $\sigma(X)=1+X$ except in the $s=1.8$ design, where
$\sigma(X)=1+1.5X$, and
\[
(s,g)\in
\{(1.2,0.3),(1.5,0.1),(1.5,0.5),(1.8,0.3),
(2,0.1),(1.5,1),(1.5,3)\}.
\]
The remaining designs have $p=5$, potential outcomes that share their noise, and
\[
Y_i(0)=m(X_i)+\sigma(X_i)u_i,
\qquad
Y_i(1)=Y_i(0)+1+\gamma^*X_{i1},
\qquad
u_i\sim N(0,1),
\]
with $m(x)=0.9\sum_jx_j$ unless stated. They differ in the covariate law,
the noise scale and the slope $\gamma^*$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Gaussian}: $X_i\sim N(0,I_5)$, $\sigma\equiv1$ and
$\gamma^*=0.3$; the large-heterogeneity Gaussian row instead has
$X_i\sim N(0,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma_{jk}=0.5^{|j-k|}$ and $\gamma^*=3$.
\item \emph{Misspecified, $G=0$}: $X_i\sim N(0,\Sigma)$ with
$\Sigma_{jk}=0.5^{|j-k|}$, $\sigma\equiv1$, $\gamma^*=0$, and
$m(x)=0.9\sum_jx_j+0.20(x_1^2-1)+0.15x_1x_2$, so that the working model is
misspecified in both arms.
\item \emph{Skewed, het., $G=0$}: independent coordinates, the first a
centered $\mathrm{LogNormal}(0,1.1^2)$ and the others standard normal, mixed
by the Cholesky factor of the matrix $0.35^{|j-k|}$ and then standardized to
unit variances; $\sigma(X)=1+0.5|X_{i1}|+0.25\|X_i\|$ and $\gamma^*=0$.
\item \emph{Heavy-tailed, het.}: the same construction with
$\mathrm{LogNormal}(0,1.5^2)$ and
$\sigma(X)=1+0.75|X_{i1}|+0.25\|X_i\|$; $\gamma^*=0.5$ in the
small-heterogeneity row and $\gamma^*=3$ in the large-heterogeneity row.
\item \emph{Rare extreme values}: $X_{i1}=\sqrt{(1-\rho)/\rho}$ with probability $\rho=0.02$ and $-\sqrt{\rho/(1-\rho)}$ otherwise, $X_{i2},\ldots,X_{i5}\sim N(0,1)$,
$\sigma\equiv1$ and $\gamma^*=1$. With $250$ units per arm, an arm contains no rare unit with probability about $0.6\%$, in which case the interacted design is rank deficient and IREG gives no interval (\gz{jk:rare:rankdef}\% of replications), and exactly one rare unit, which then has leverage one in its arm's fit, with probability about $3\%$.
\end{itemize}
